Time and observation

We notice time most clearly when something changes. A shadow crosses a room, a train reaches a platform, a familiar voice becomes a memory. Yet none of these experiences tells us quite what has passed.

A clock offers a useful answer to a practical question. It compares one change with another: the movement of a hand, the swing of a pendulum, the vibration of an atom. To describe time, we begin by comparing changes. Agreement between clocks makes the comparison dependable, but it does not settle every question about what they measure.

Measuring time and understanding time may be different questions. We can count the hours of a journey with great accuracy and still wonder why a short wait feels longer than a busy afternoon.

The observer belongs to this story too. An observation takes place somewhere, with an instrument, under particular conditions. When two observers disagree, it helps to ask what each could see and how each made a measurement.

Perhaps the first step is to leave the question open long enough to notice its parts. Duration, order, memory, and change are close companions. They need not be identical to be worth considering together.
